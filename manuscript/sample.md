*This little book is a sample manuscript. It was written for the pipeline
that builds it, in the manner of the plain sea stories of a century ago.*

# The Keeper

There stood, upon the farthest rock of the northern shore, a lighthouse
which the fishing people called the Lamp at Greywater Point. It was not a
handsome tower. The salt had eaten the paint from its door, and the gulls
had made free with its gallery; but for forty winters its light had gone
round without once failing, and the boats had come home by it.

The keeper was an old man who spoke seldom and smiled less. He trimmed the
wick at dusk, wound the great clockwork at midnight, and wrote in his log
each morning the same four words: **Light kept. All well.**

The village thought him a hard man. They did not know that he counted the
boats out every morning, and counted them in again every night, and did not
sleep until the two numbers agreed.

> A lamp asks nothing of the ship it saves.
> It only burns, and the ship goes by.

So the old people of the shore were used to say, and so the keeper lived.

# The Storm

In the autumn of the great gale there came to the lighthouse a girl of
twelve years, the keeper's granddaughter, whose mother had been taken by a
fever in the south. She brought with her one small trunk and a great many
questions.

"Why does it turn?" she asked on the first night.

"So that a sailor may know *this* light from every other," said the keeper.

"And if it should stop?"

"It does not stop."

* * *

It stopped on the ninth of November.

The gale came out of the north-east a little after sunset. By eight o'clock
the sea was breaking over the lower rocks, and by nine the keeper, going
down for oil, set his foot upon a wet stair and fell the height of a room.
The girl found him at the bottom with his leg turned under him and his face
the colour of the lamp glass.

"The clockwork," he said. "It must be wound at midnight, or the light
stands still. A standing light is a false light. They will take it for the
harbour mark and steer upon the reef."

There were, that night, eleven boats still out.

The girl had never climbed the tower alone. She did so now, with the
lantern in her teeth and both hands upon the rail, and the wind howling in
the stair like a living thing. What she did there may be set down briefly:

1. She found the winding handle, which was as long as her arm.
2. She could not turn it by her strength alone.
3. She hung her whole weight upon it, and it turned.

One hundred and forty turns the clockwork wanted. She gave it every one.

# The Morning

At dawn the wind fell. The keeper, who had lain all night at the foot of
the stair with a blanket over him, heard the gallery door open far above,
and small boots upon the iron.

"Count them," he called. "Count them in."

There was a long silence. Then her voice came down the tower, thin and
clear:

"Eleven."

The keeper shut his eyes.

They carried him to the village that afternoon, and the bone-setter said he
would walk again by spring. The fishing people, who had thought him hard,
stood in the lane with their caps in their hands. But it was the girl they
looked at.

That evening she opened the log, and under forty years of the same four
words she wrote, in a round and careful hand:

> Light kept.\
> All well.\
> Eleven out, and eleven home.

And the lamp went round.
